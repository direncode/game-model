SOURCE: Port-Royal Grammar (Grammaire generale et raisonnee, 1660) by Antoine Arnauld and Claude Lancelot
CITATION: Arnauld & Lancelot 1660; Chomsky "Cartesian Linguistics" (1966); scholarly summaries

The Port-Royal Grammar, formally titled "Grammaire generale et raisonnee contenant les fondements de l'art de parler expliques d'une maniere claire et naturelle" (General and Rational Grammar Containing the Fundamentals of the Art of Speaking Explained in a Clear and Natural Manner), was published anonymously in 1660 by Antoine Arnauld and Claude Lancelot at the Port-Royal abbey near Paris. It represents the Cartesian rationalist approach to language: the view that grammar reflects the universal structure of human thought, and that the diversity of human languages is superficial variation on a common underlying logical system.

THE RATIONALIST FRAMEWORK

Arnauld and Lancelot argued that language is fundamentally an instrument for expressing judgments. Every sentence, in their view, corresponds to a mental proposition with a subject, a copula, and a predicate. The surface form of sentences varies across languages, but the underlying logical structure is universal. The aim of general grammar is to identify this universal logical structure and explain how each individual language maps it onto specific syntactic patterns.

This framework treats grammatical categories as derivative from mental operations. Nouns express ideas of things (substances or their accidents). Verbs express judgments or assertions about things. Adjectives express qualities. Adverbs modify verbs as adjectives modify nouns. The grammatical part-of-speech system reflects the inventory of mental operations.

THE DEEP/SURFACE DISTINCTION

The Port-Royal grammarians introduced what Noam Chomsky would later identify as a deep-surface distinction, though without using those terms. They observed that a single logical proposition can be expressed by multiple surface sentences. The proposition "the invisible God has created the visible world" contains three judgments: God is invisible, God has created the world, the world is visible. The surface sentence collapses three underlying propositions into one, using relative clauses and adjectival modification to embed judgments within judgments.

The grammarians explicitly stated that the "complete expression" of a sentence requires unpacking it into its component judgments. The surface form is a compressed representation; the deep form is a set of propositions joined by logical operations. This anticipates the transformational grammar framework in which deep structures (propositions) are mapped to surface structures (spoken sentences) via transformations (relative clause formation, adjectival reduction, etc.).

Chomsky explicitly credited the Port-Royal grammarians in his 1966 book "Cartesian Linguistics" with anticipating the generative grammar program. While later scholars have debated whether Chomsky's reading was historically accurate, the textual evidence in the Port-Royal Grammar does support a deep-surface distinction in some form: sentences have a logical structure that is not identical to their surface form, and grammar must account for the mapping.

UNIVERSAL FEATURES AND LANGUAGE-SPECIFIC VARIATION

The Port-Royal Grammar treated French as its primary object of study but compared it frequently with Latin, Greek, and Hebrew. The comparative approach led Arnauld and Lancelot to identify features that appear in all languages (every language has nouns and verbs, every language can express judgments, every language can form complex sentences from simple ones) and features that vary (word order, case marking, verbal morphology).

This distinction between universal and particular features foreshadows Chomsky's principles-and-parameters framework in the 1980s, where universal grammar provides a set of principles shared by all languages, and individual languages differ only in parameter settings. The Port-Royal grammarians were not working with a principles-and-parameters framework in the modern technical sense, but their distinction between what is universal in grammar and what is language-specific is structurally parallel.

COMPOSITIONALITY

The Port-Royal Grammar assumes that the meaning of a complex sentence is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. This is the principle of compositionality, now central to formal semantics. Arnauld and Lancelot did not state it as a theoretical principle, but they assumed it in their analyses. A sentence is decomposed into subject, copula, and predicate; the meanings of these parts combine according to logical rules to produce the meaning of the whole.

Compositionality is one of the most important ideas in 20th-century formal semantics. Richard Montague's 1970s work on Montague grammar made compositionality precise using lambda calculus and type theory. The Port-Royal grammarians were working with an informal version of the same idea: grammatical structure mirrors logical structure, and meaning is computed by combining meanings according to grammatical structure.

MODALITY AND EMBEDDING

The grammar handles embedded clauses and modal expressions with care. Arnauld and Lancelot recognized that embedded clauses can themselves contain embedded clauses, producing sentences of arbitrary depth. They did not explicitly state a recursive rule, but their analysis implicitly treats embedding as a recursive operation.

Modal expressions — "I believe that X," "It is necessary that X," "It is possible that X" — are treated as operators on propositions. The Port-Royal grammarians noted that these operators change the truth conditions of the embedded proposition in systematic ways. This anticipates modal logic, which was not formalized until the 1910s-1950s work of C. I. Lewis, Rudolf Carnap, and Saul Kripke.

THE LEGACY

The Port-Royal Grammar was influential throughout the 18th century but fell into disrepute in the 19th century as historical and comparative linguistics took over. It was rediscovered by Noam Chomsky in the 1960s as part of his argument that generative grammar is continuous with a long rationalist tradition in linguistic theory. Chomsky's "Cartesian Linguistics" (1966) placed the Port-Royal Grammar at the center of this tradition.

Modern scholars have debated Chomsky's historical claims. Some argue that the Port-Royal grammarians' rationalism is not as close to modern generative grammar as Chomsky suggested. Others argue that the deep-surface distinction, the universality claim, and the compositionality assumption are all genuinely present in the 1660 text. The textual analysis supports the structural parallel, even if the exact formal apparatus is different.

For computational historiography, the Port-Royal Grammar represents a 17th-century attempt to formalize linguistic structure as the expression of universal logical thought. It predates formal logic by 200 years and generative grammar by 300 years. The specific parallels — deep vs surface structure, universal vs language-specific features, compositionality, treatment of embedding — are the kinds of structural matches that BTUT-style detection should find when comparing historical grammar texts with modern linguistic theory.
