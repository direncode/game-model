SOURCE: Wilhelm von Humboldt, "On Language" / "Ueber die Verschiedenheit des menschlichen Sprachbaues" (1836)
CITATION: Humboldt 1836; English translation by Peter Heath (1988) or Harris (1999); Chomsky "Cartesian Linguistics" (1966)

Wilhelm von Humboldt's posthumously published "Ueber die Verschiedenheit des menschlichen Sprachbaues und ihren Einfluss auf die geistige Entwickelung des Menschengeschlechts" ("On the Diversity of Human Language Structure and Its Influence on the Mental Development of Mankind", 1836) is one of the foundational texts of general linguistics. Humboldt was a Prussian philosopher, diplomat, and linguist who studied an extraordinary range of languages including Basque, Native American languages, Egyptian, Chinese, Sanskrit, and Javanese. His work synthesized philosophical reflection on the nature of language with detailed empirical comparison of diverse linguistic structures.

ENERGEIA VERSUS ERGON

Humboldt's most famous conceptual distinction is between language as energeia (activity) and language as ergon (product). Language, Humboldt argued, is not a finished object that can be described statically. It is a continuous, creative activity of the mind. A dictionary and a grammar describe the dead traces of this activity, not the activity itself. Language is fundamentally generative: each speaker produces novel sentences by applying an inherited system of rules to new content.

This distinction is Aristotelian in origin (Aristotle used energeia for activity or actuality, ergon for product or result), but Humboldt applied it to language in a way that anticipates modern theoretical linguistics. The view that language is a generative activity, not a catalog of sentences, is the foundation of Noam Chomsky's generative grammar program in the 20th century. Chomsky explicitly credits Humboldt for the phrase "the infinite use of finite means," which became one of the central slogans of generative grammar.

INFINITE USE OF FINITE MEANS

Humboldt wrote that language "must make infinite use of finite means" — a phrase that Chomsky repeatedly cited as anticipating the central insight of generative grammar. The idea is that a finite inventory of lexical items and a finite inventory of grammatical rules can produce an infinite set of sentences, because the rules can be applied recursively. A speaker can embed a clause within a clause within a clause, building arbitrarily complex structures from simple parts.

This is the recursive or generative property of human language. It was not formalized mathematically until the mid-20th century, with the work of Emil Post on formal systems, Alonzo Church on lambda calculus, Alan Turing on computability, and Noam Chomsky on phrase structure grammars. Humboldt did not provide a formal definition of recursion, but he identified the property and insisted on its centrality.

INNER FORM

A third central concept in Humboldt's thought is "innere Sprachform" (inner language form). The inner form of a language is the characteristic way in which that language organizes thought — the conceptual schemes embedded in its lexicon, its grammar, its idiomatic constructions. Humboldt believed that each language has its own inner form, reflecting the spirit of the people who speak it, and that learning a language means acquiring this inner form.

The inner form concept has been interpreted in various ways. Some scholars see it as an early version of the Sapir-Whorf linguistic relativity hypothesis: the idea that the language you speak shapes the way you think. Others see it as a precursor to cognitive linguistics, where grammatical structures are seen as reflections of conceptual structures. The concept has also been compared to the modern notion of "grammaticalization patterns" — the tendency of languages to encode certain conceptual distinctions grammatically and others lexically.

WORLDVIEW AND LINGUISTIC RELATIVITY

Humboldt explicitly argued that language shapes worldview. He wrote that "man lives with the world about him, principally, indeed exclusively, as language presents it to him." Different languages present the world differently, emphasizing different distinctions, grouping concepts differently, structuring temporal and spatial relations differently. Learning a new language, Humboldt said, means adopting a new worldview.

This claim — that linguistic structure shapes cognitive structure — was developed in the 20th century by Franz Boas, Edward Sapir, and Benjamin Lee Whorf into what is now called the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis or linguistic relativity. Modern research on linguistic relativity has produced mixed but meaningful results: certain domains (color terms, spatial reference systems, grammatical number) do show measurable effects of linguistic structure on cognition, while other domains (core logical operations, basic perception) do not. The modern field of cognitive linguistics is a direct descendant of Humboldt's view that grammar reflects cognitive organization.

TYPOLOGICAL CLASSIFICATION

Humboldt proposed a typological classification of languages based on their morphological structure. He distinguished four main types: isolating languages (like Chinese) where words do not change form and grammatical relations are expressed by word order; agglutinative languages (like Turkish) where affixes are clearly separable and each expresses one grammatical category; inflecting languages (like Latin or Sanskrit) where affixes are fused and each expresses multiple categories simultaneously; incorporating languages (like many Native American languages) where what would be a sentence in other languages is expressed as a single complex word.

This typological classification has been refined but remains foundational in modern morphological typology. It also anticipates modern scales of morphological complexity, such as the synthesis index and the fusion index, which are used in computational linguistics to characterize languages quantitatively.

LANGUAGE AND THOUGHT

Humboldt insisted that language and thought are inseparable. He wrote that "language is the formative organ of thought." Thinking in a language is not translating pre-linguistic thoughts into words; it is thinking in a language from the beginning. The grammatical and lexical resources of the language shape the thoughts that can be easily expressed in it.

This view has been defended by cognitive scientists working on the interface between language and cognition. Lera Boroditsky, Daniel Slobin, and others have published empirical evidence that grammatical structure influences attention, memory, and categorization. The modern field of psycholinguistics has moved away from a naive Whorfianism toward a more nuanced view in which language influences but does not determine thought, while preserving Humboldt's core insight that language and thought are intertwined.

HUMBOLDT AND MODERN GENERATIVE GRAMMAR

Chomsky's 1966 "Cartesian Linguistics" placed Humboldt in a line of rationalist thinkers who anticipated generative grammar. The key Humboldt ideas Chomsky cited are: the view of language as a rule-governed creative activity (energeia), the infinite use of finite means, the universality of grammar underlying surface diversity, and the creative aspect of normal language use.

Later scholars have debated Chomsky's historical reading. Some argue that Humboldt's rationalism is not as close to Chomsky's as Chomsky suggested, and that Humboldt's emphasis on language diversity and the role of language in shaping thought is in tension with Chomsky's emphasis on universal grammar and the modularity of language. Others defend Chomsky's reading as capturing the generative aspect of Humboldt's thought while noting that Humboldt had other concerns as well.

Regardless of how we adjudicate this dispute, the textual parallels between Humboldt and modern generative grammar are real: the generative insight, the recursive nature of grammar, the universality claim, the distinction between language as activity and language as product, and the view that grammar reflects deep cognitive organization. All of these are present in the 1836 text and would be formalized in the 20th century.

For computational historiography, Humboldt represents a 19th-century attempt to capture, in philosophical language, the central properties that 20th-century linguistics would later formalize: generativity, recursion, universality, and the cognitive basis of grammar. The specific vocabulary he used — energeia, ergon, innere Sprachform, infinite use of finite means — maps to modern technical concepts in a way that BTUT-style structural analysis should detect.
