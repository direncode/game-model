SYMMETRIC BLOCK CIPHER SPECIFICATION
ADVANCED ENCRYPTION STANDARD (AES) / RIJNDAEL
NIST FIPS PUB 197
Designers: Joan Daemen, Vincent Rijmen
Standardized: 2001 (after NIST AES competition 1997-2000)

ABSTRACT
The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) is a symmetric block cipher selected by NIST as the successor to DES. AES operates on 128-bit blocks using a key of 128, 192, or 256 bits. The cipher uses a substitution-permutation network with 10, 12, or 14 rounds depending on key size. AES is a SYMMETRIC cipher: the same key is used for encryption and decryption.

BACKGROUND

Symmetric (or secret-key) cryptography requires both parties to share a secret key in advance. This is in contrast to public-key (asymmetric) cryptography where parties can establish secure communication without prior secret sharing. Symmetric ciphers are computationally efficient and suitable for bulk data encryption, but they require a separate key distribution mechanism.

The Data Encryption Standard (DES) was the previous standard, designed in the 1970s with a 56-bit key. By the late 1990s, DES was considered insecure due to its short key length, motivating NIST to select a successor. After a 4-year competition involving cryptanalysis of multiple candidate ciphers, the Rijndael cipher (designed by Daemen and Rijmen) was selected as AES in 2000 and standardized as FIPS PUB 197 in 2001.

DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

AES is a substitution-permutation network (SPN) block cipher. Unlike Feistel ciphers (DES), AES processes the entire block in each round through a series of byte-level substitutions, byte permutations, and column mixing operations. The design aims for:
- Resistance to all known cryptanalytic attacks (linear, differential, integral, etc.)
- High performance on a wide range of hardware platforms
- Simplicity and elegance of mathematical structure
- Adequate security margins

MATHEMATICAL STRUCTURE

AES operates on a 4x4 matrix of bytes called the state. Each round consists of four operations:

1. SubBytes: Each byte is replaced by its image under a fixed S-box. The S-box is constructed from the multiplicative inverse in the finite field GF(2^8) followed by an affine transformation.

2. ShiftRows: Each row of the state is cyclically shifted by a different offset (0, 1, 2, 3 bytes for rows 0-3).

3. MixColumns: Each column is multiplied by a fixed polynomial in GF(2^8)[x]/(x^4 + 1). This provides diffusion.

4. AddRoundKey: The state is XORed with a round key derived from the cipher key via the key schedule.

The cipher applies these operations in 10, 12, or 14 rounds for 128, 192, and 256-bit keys respectively. The final round omits the MixColumns step.

KEY SCHEDULE

The key schedule expands the cipher key into a sequence of round keys. For AES-128, the original 128-bit key is expanded into 11 round keys (one for the initial AddRoundKey and one for each of the 10 rounds). The expansion uses a combination of byte rotation, S-box substitution, and constant XOR.

SECURITY MODEL

AES security relies on the computational difficulty of distinguishing the cipher's output from random for any unknown key. The best known attacks are:
- Brute force: 2^128 operations for AES-128 (infeasible)
- Biclique attacks: ~2^126 operations (theoretical, no practical impact)
- Side-channel attacks: implementation-specific, not fundamental to the cipher

The mathematical foundation is the substitution-permutation network design and the algebraic structure of GF(2^8). AES does NOT rely on number-theoretic problems like factoring or discrete logarithms.

DISTINCTION FROM PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTOGRAPHY

AES is fundamentally different from public-key cryptosystems:

1. Symmetric: Same key for encryption and decryption (vs. RSA, DH which have public/private key pairs)

2. Computational basis: AES security relies on the design of substitution-permutation operations and finite field arithmetic, NOT on number-theoretic hardness assumptions like factoring or discrete logarithms.

3. Performance: AES is orders of magnitude faster than RSA or other public-key systems for bulk data encryption. AES-128 can encrypt at gigabytes per second on modern hardware.

4. Key length: AES uses 128-256 bit keys to achieve security, while public-key systems require thousands of bits.

5. Quantum resistance: Grover's algorithm provides only quadratic speedup against AES, requiring 2^64 operations for AES-128 (still infeasible). AES is considered quantum-resistant. RSA and DH are NOT quantum-resistant due to Shor's algorithm.

USE IN PRACTICE

AES is used as the symmetric component in hybrid cryptosystems. Public-key cryptography (RSA, DH, ECDH) is used to establish or exchange a session key, and AES is then used for the bulk encryption of data using that session key. This combines the convenience of public-key key distribution with the efficiency of symmetric encryption.

This is a SYMMETRIC BLOCK CIPHER. It serves as a control sample for the cryptography domain experiment because it represents a fundamentally different mathematical structure than public-key cryptosystems. Where public-key systems (RSA, DH, NTRU) rely on hard mathematical problems in number theory or lattices, AES relies on the design of substitution-permutation networks and the algebraic structure of small finite fields.
